### What the figures mean for the design

- 71.7% of Peruvian workers are in units of 1 to 10 people, and 88.6% of those are informal. The tool is built for that scale: no accounts, no server and no staff. It does the reading and sorting; the operator decides.
- 89.2% of rural households have a mobile phone and 27.1% have internet service. The daily work has to run on a phone, offline, after one download.
- Quechua is the mother tongue of 55.2% of the Cusco region's population aged 5 and over, and the model fails on it (see below). The tool reports that failure instead of hiding it.
- UN Tourism reports 2 to 3 companions per traveller with a disability, so a barrier on a walking route can affect a whole group. The route view shows where street photos suggest steps or kerbs, and a person confirms each one.

## The Qorikancha route

The main example: a walking route in Cusco from the Plaza de Armas to the Qorikancha ticket booth, prepared once from public street photos and map data. Figures from the route's own record:

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Route | 593.9 m on foot, from the public Valhalla router on OpenStreetMap data; 60 stretches of up to 10 m |
| Photos | 403 Mapillary photo records by 7 contributors, captured between July 2015 and December 2023. They cover 58 of the 60 stretches; 20 m has no photos |
| 3D | Partial 3D from VGGT-1B-Commercial, placed for 22 of 30 photo spots |
| Outlines | SAM 3 scanned 116 photo views; the 51 outlines on the route were kept, and 73 more than 3 m from it were left out |
| Findings | 52: the 51 photo outlines and 1 OpenStreetMap tag |
| Possible barriers | 8, on 6 stretches: 6 steps outlines, 1 kerb with no ramp found, and the OpenStreetMap tag "5 steps, no handrail, no ramp" on Calle Loreto |
| Checked by a person | None in the recorded data |

- **"No barrier seen in photos" means only that.** A stretch without a flagged barrier has not been measured. Nothing in the route record says how wide or steep a stretch is, or whether a wheelchair can pass, and the route view claims none of it.
- **Two kinds of AI.** VGGT and SAM 3 ran once on a GPU workstation when the route was prepared; their output is recorded and unverified. On the phone, only the small message model runs. A person confirms or dismisses every finding.
- **Credits in every view and video shot.** A photo must show its Mapillary contributor and CC BY-SA 4.0. A map must show "© OpenStreetMap contributors" (ODbL 1.0).
- **What is published.** The repository ships a small package of this route (`public/places/qorikancha`, 2,189,164 bytes): the route, its 60 stretches, all 52 findings, 50 photo records and 27 credited photo crops, shared under CC BY-SA 4.0. The full set of 403 photos and the 3D stay on the development machine and are not in the repository or the build.

## Noor's farm (secondary example)

An authored coffee farm tasting terrace in La Convención, labeled synthetic. It has 17 named features in Spanish and English, with aliases in English, Spanish, Korean and Quechua (unreviewed), and every dimension is invented. Its path check uses an illustrative 0.9 m square envelope on one flat level, which is not a wheelchair standard; slopes, steps and surface firmness are not modelled. The model's held-out evaluation uses messages about this farm.

## What the data does not cover

- **Real visitors.** No message comes from a real visitor, and no person wrote any of the examples. There is no sample of how real visitors write: length, spelling, mixed languages or several topics in one message.
- **Native speakers.** No native speaker has reviewed the English, Spanish, Korean or Quechua messages, the Korean and Quechua aliases, or the app's pre-written Korean reply. How natural or correct the machine-translated Quechua is remains unknown.
- **Quechua in the model.** The encoder's model card says it supports the 100 languages of XLM-R and warns that low-resource languages may see performance degradation. The [XLM-R card](https://huggingface.co/FacebookAI/xlm-roberta-base) lists English, Spanish and Korean, not Quechua. The encoder's own training data includes NLLB translation pairs, so some exposure to Quechua cannot be ruled out. On the 22 held-out Quechua messages the model took most for praise, put the right feature first for 5 of the 16 that name one, and gave 9 confident answers, only 1 of them right. A language check added after that run now answers *Not sure* for messages that do not look like English, Spanish or Korean: all 22 Quechua messages, and no English, Spanish or Korean held-out message. It makes the tool abstain on Quechua, not understand it, and it also catches very short messages without common words.
- **Route messages.** The preregistered evaluation uses messages about the farm. A later set of 44 synthetic messages about the Qorikancha walk, scored once with the farm-trained heads, put the right spot first for 28 of 31 English, Spanish and Korean messages, but got the issue type right for only 8 of 28 problems, so the route screen does not show it. These route messages are also model-written and unreviewed.
- **The operator's own language.** The interface is not offered in Quechua.
- **Measurements.** Nothing has been measured on site. The farm's dimensions are invented, and the route has photos and partial 3D, not measurements: neither says how wide or steep a path is. The farm's path check ignores slope, mud, step height and turning space, and nothing here covers needs other than passage width, such as low vision or hearing.
- **Recorded imagery.** The route photos date from 2015 to 2023 and may not show current conditions, and 20 m of the route has none. The outlines are SAM 3's own marks and none is checked by a person in the recorded data. The 3D covers 22 of 30 photo spots, and outlines more than 3 m from the route were left out.
- **Phones.** No phone has been measured: the speeds in [language](language.md) come from the development Mac (about 29 ms per message), and a sixfold CPU slowdown emulated in Chromium gave a median of 185 ms. On iPhone, Safari deletes a site's stored data after seven days of browsing without a visit unless the site was added to the home screen, so the model may need downloading again.
- **Operators.** No study with real operators: time saved, correction effort and trust are unmeasured.
- **Tourism figures.** MINCETUR counts border arrivals by country of residence. It does not count visitors on a given route or tour, the languages they write in, or visitors with disabilities. These sources give no figure for Korean-speaking visitors in Cusco.
- **Firms and informality.** INEI's informality figures cover all sectors, not tourism alone. The Enterprise Surveys exclude informal firms and firms with fewer than five employees, which is where a small tour operator sits.
- **Place.** Coffee figures are for the Cusco region, not La Convención province. The rural connectivity figures describe an operator like Noor, not the historic centre of Cusco, and INEI's phone figures do not separate smartphones from basic phones.
- **Disability figures.** WHO and INEI measure disability differently, so their figures are not comparable. Neither counts visitors with disabilities at Peruvian tourism sites, and UN Tourism's 85 million is a regional estimate of potential demand.
